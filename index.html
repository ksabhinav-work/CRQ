CRQ step 1 · Scenario definition
Every quantification starts with a well-formed scenario: not "quantum risk", but a sentence with defined parts.
Actor & path
An adversary recording SWIT's classically encrypted traffic today, to decrypt the day a quantum computer exists
Asset
Applicant KYC files, financials and personal data on the single-window portal
Loss driver
Confidentiality, realised retroactively at Q-Day
Horizon & unit
Multi-decade · measured in records exposed, not rupees
The race has three runners: Q-Day, your migration, and the clock on how long the data must stay secret. Nobody knows when Q-Day is, and the method's quiet trick is that you will never need to.

CRQ step 2 · Decomposition and calibrated estimation

Four honest ranges

You cannot estimate "our quantum risk" directly, and CRQ never asks anyone to. The unknowable whole is decomposed into the parts that actually generate the loss, and each part gets a calibrated range: an interval the expert would genuinely bet on, with its anchoring evidence written down.

Note the quality gradient, which is itself method: one input is barely an estimate (corpus data is a database query), one has internal test evidence, one is external expert elicitation, one is honest near-ignorance with the widest interval. A 90% interval is a tested claim, not a feeling: calibration training, a half-day exercise, brings experts to the point where their 90% intervals really do contain the truth about 90% of the time.

CRQ step 2, continued · Estimates become distributions

Q-Day becomes a bowl of chits

A range is a statement; a distribution is that statement made drawable. Write thousands of chits: many say years near 2037, some say the early 2030s, a few say the 2050s. The bowl now is the expert belief, in physical form, and arguing about the single true year becomes visibly pointless. Draw from it.

Each draw drops a dot on the timeline. Keep drawing and the belief takes shape: a hill peaking in the late 2030s with a long tail into the 2050s.
In CRQ termsEach interval is fitted to a probability distribution, which is just a standard curve shape: PERT for min / likely / max judgements, lognormal for skewed never-negative quantities. The shapes are conventions, swappable and testable, pinned so the curve agrees exactly with the expert's stated bounds. Nothing is added to their statement; the curve only fills in the shape between the bounds they gave.
3
CRQ step 3 · Monte Carlo, one trial at a time

One possible future, in the ledger

Play out a single future under the current plan, which we'll call the roadmap: migration starts in 2031. The clerk draws one chit from every bowl, plus a fresh secrecy-lifetime chit for each year's harvest, then simply checks who won the race. Data harvested before migration completes is decryptable at Q-Day; a year's harvest only stops mattering if Q-Day arrives after that cohort has aged out of sensitivity. And because systems migrate progressively, the harvestable share of traffic tapers down across the migration window itself.

The clerk is waiting.
The clerk's ledger · one row per simulated future
In CRQ termsEach ledger row is one Monte Carlo trial: a draw from every input distribution (the secrecy clock drawn afresh for every yearly harvest cohort), pushed through the scenario's arithmetic, and recorded. The simulation adds no randomness of its own; it propagates the uncertainty the experts already admitted to, instead of collapsing every range to a midpoint and pretending the result is exact.

Convergence · the running P95 estimate
Watch the tail estimate wobble and settle. Monte Carlo error shrinks with the square root of the trial count, so ten times the precision costs a hundred times the runs, and tail percentiles wobble longest. This is why 1,000 futures is a sketch and 20,000 is a measurement, and why the two buttons exist.
In CRQ termsThe pile of trials is the loss distribution. "Futures with exposure" is the loss event probability over the horizon; the orange line is the 95th percentile (P95), the value only 1 future in 20 exceeds; the unit is operational (records) rather than monetary, a legitimate CRQ choice when money is not what the institution actually cares about. The Mosca check below is validation against a hand calculation, the discipline that keeps a model honest.
Sensitivity · which range is worth refining?

Pin one input at its low bound and rerun, then at its high bound and rerun, leaving every other input fully uncertain. The bar spans the resulting P95s for the roadmap plan; the orange line is the all-uncertain base case. The widest bar is where better evidence buys the most.

In CRQ termsThis is one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis, the gateway to value of information: the next rupee of analysis effort belongs to the input with the widest bar, because tightening it moves the answer most. Note the trap it defuses: the widest bar typically drives the level of the loss, but the Act 5 ranking can be insensitive to it when that input scales every strategy equally. Levels tell you how big; rankings tell you what to do; they have different sensitivities.
5
CRQ step 5 · Decision analysis: comparing risk treatments

The decision: four strategies, same bowl

This is the step the entire method exists for. Four strategies are on the table, and in a qualitative discussion all four sound defensible, including the sophisticated one: surely we'll get warning. Replay the same twenty thousand futures under each and they separate immediately. Note what you are about to see: no strategy buys zero, because anything harvested before migration completes remains a future liability. What you are choosing is the size of the eventual exposure, and whether your migration finishes before the deadline you don't control.

Wait for proof

Start only when a quantum computer is publicly demonstrated · crash programme compressing migration to 0.5 to 0.7 of normal duration · Rs. 12 to 18 cr

The intuitive plan: why spend before the threat is real? The model even grants the crash compression the premium buys. But everything carried over classical crypto until that day has already been harvested, and no compression rescues it. Cryptographic procrastination.

Start at warning

Begin when a credible early signal appears, granted generously as 2 to 5 years' notice before Q-Day · Rs. 6 cr, start date unknowable

The sophisticated objection: we'll see it coming. The model concedes perfect warning and watch how little it buys: notice helps the migration clock and does nothing for the years of traffic already sitting in the adversary's vault.

Roadmap 2031

Begin in five years, normal pace · Rs. 6 cr from 2031

The institutional default: it is on the roadmap, budgeted for later. Five more years of fully harvestable operation, then a 2-to-8-year migration racing the bowl.

Start now

Begin in 2026, normal pace · Rs. 6 cr from 2026

Same money, spent earlier. The vulnerable window shrinks to the migration itself, and tapers as systems move. Hybrid rollout, crypto-agility, inventory first.

What to remember. The inputs were four honest ranges; the machine was a clerk racing three clocks through a bowl of chits. You never needed to know when Q-Day is: the strategies separated under every plausible Q-Day at once, and even a generous early-warning assumption barely moved the outcome, because warning cannot un-harvest the past. Starting early doesn't buy safety; it buys a smaller bomb and a race you actually win. And the method that showed you this was nothing more exotic than CRQ doing its job: subjectivity written down, disciplined, propagated, and made decisive.
